# Credits and sources

The text of this handbook is original. Its structure and many of its ideas come from published work on lab handbooks and from groups that shared theirs.

## Lab handbook guidance

Tendler BC, Welland M, Miller KL, The WIN Handbook Team. Research culture: why every lab needs a handbook. eLife 12, e88853 (2023). https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.88853. Published under CC BY 4.0. The grouping of content into roles and expectations, group culture, and member development comes from this article, as do the advice to write the handbook as a group, the point that a short finished handbook beats a long unfinished one, the accountability route that includes the lab head, and the lesson that vague wording about working hours gets read in different ways.

Heidt A. Lab morale got you down? Try a handbook. Nature 650, 512 to 513 (2026). https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-026-00390-6. The early authorship policy, the explicit open science expectations, the conduct briefing for visits to partner sites, and the emphasis on keeping the document easy to maintain follow examples reported there.

SAFE Labs handbook, https://safelabs.info/home/safe-labs-handbook/. Its split into policies, teams and careers informed how the chapters here are grouped. No text was copied.

## Standards referred to in the chapters

Authorship criteria in chapter 9 follow the recommendations of the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors. Contribution roles follow the CRediT taxonomy (ANSI/NISO Z39.104-2022). Data practice in chapter 8 follows the FAIR principles (Wilkinson et al., Scientific Data, 2016). Animal research reporting in chapter 7 follows the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.
