# Mission and values

## What the lab is for

We study how the human nervous system develops and how medicines and environmental chemicals change it. We hold ourselves to research that other people can check, reuse and build on. We also train researchers, and we count how well people are trained as a result of the lab, alongside papers and grants. Current projects are listed at https://zosenlab.org.

## What good science means here

A result is only as good as the record of how it was obtained. If a colleague cannot repeat your analysis from your files, the work is not finished.

Negative and null results get written up, filed and discussed. An experiment that did not work is information, and the next person needs it.

We say how sure we are. An observation, a trend and a finding are different things, and our slides, figures and papers should show which one we have.

A claim rests on more than one readout. Before we call something a finding, we look at it in at least two independent ways, and we say so when we have only one.

Write down what would change your mind before you see the data: which result would make us continue, change course or stop.

The people who donated cells and tissue made this work possible. Their consent sets the limits of what we do with it.

Speed matters, especially for competitive projects. It never trades against the points above.

## Behaviours we expect from everyone

Tell people when you have made a mistake, on the day you find it. A mislabelled tube, a miscalibrated pipette, a bug in an analysis script: all of these happen. Nobody is penalised for an honest error that is reported. Hiding one is a serious problem, because other people build on your work.

Ask early. Nobody expects a new member to know how the freezers are organised or how to book an instrument. Nobody expects the lab head to know every detail of your project either, so tell them what you need to know.

Credit people specifically. Say who made the reagent, who wrote the script, who had the idea that fixed the assay. Do it in lab meetings, in papers, and in acknowledgements.

Argue about the science as hard as you like, and keep it about the data and not the person.

Treat other people's time as limited. Send a message that says what you need and by when. Come to meetings prepared. Do not leave shared equipment in a state someone else has to fix.

## What the lab owes you

Clear expectations in writing, honest and timely feedback, fair credit for your work, a safe place to work, and support for your career in whichever direction you want to take it, inside or outside academia. [Chapter 3](03-roles-and-expectations.md) turns these into specific commitments, and [chapter 5](05-conduct-and-raising-concerns.md) says what to do if they are not met.
