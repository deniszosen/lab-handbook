# Authorship and credit

Authorship is where labs argue most. We talk about it at the start of every project, and again when things change, so that nobody finds out at submission that they were never going to be on the paper.

## Who is an author

We follow the criteria of the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors. To be an author you need all four of these:

- a substantial contribution to the conception or design of the work, or to the collection, analysis or interpretation of the data
- a contribution to drafting the paper or revising it critically for important content
- final approval of the version to be published
- agreement to be accountable for the parts of the work you did, and to help resolve questions about the rest

People who contributed but do not meet all four are named in the acknowledgements, with their contribution described. Position, seniority and funding are not contributions. Gift authorship, guest authorship and ghost authorship are not allowed.

Technicians, research staff and students are authors when they meet the criteria, exactly like postdocs and the lab head. An experienced technician who helped design an assay, ran the experiments and approved the paper is an author.

## Agreeing on it

At the start of each project, the people involved fill in the [authorship and contribution plan](../templates/authorship-contribution-plan.md): who is expected to contribute what, the likely author order, and who writes the first draft. It goes in the project README.

Revisit the plan at three points: when the paper is outlined, when the first full draft exists, and just before submission. Contributions change, and the order should follow what happened and not what was expected. With external partners, agree authorship in the written agreement described in [chapter 11](11-ethics-integrity-and-compliance.md).

## Order

The first author did the most work on the paper and usually wrote the first draft. The last author is the senior researcher who supervised and is responsible for the work. Co-first and co-senior authorship is used when contributions were equal, with a note in the paper. The corresponding author is whoever will handle the journal and the questions after publication, and may be the lab head or the first author.

Everyone who is an author gets an ORCID iD and uses it.

## Contribution statements

Each paper lists contributions using the CRediT roles: conceptualisation, data curation, formal analysis, funding acquisition, investigation, methodology, project administration, resources, software, supervision, validation, visualisation, writing of the original draft, and writing by review and editing. Authors check their own entry.

## Software, protocols and data

Software, protocols and datasets are research outputs. Someone who built an analysis pipeline, wrote a protocol that others now use, or produced a dataset that others will reuse gets a CRediT role for it (software, resources, data curation or methodology) and is named in the repository and the paper.

## Theses and papers

PhD students are first author on the papers from work they led. Chapters of a thesis that are also papers say so, and co-authors approve this. Students' theses are never held back to wait for a paper.

## Leaving the lab

Authorship earned before you leave stays yours. The lab will finish the paper, and expects to submit work from your project within twelve months of your departure. If it cannot, the lab head and you review the authorship plan together. You will get the draft, a chance to comment, and the right to decline authorship if you disagree with the content.

## Disagreements

If you disagree about authorship, start with the project plan and the contribution statements. Talk to the first author and the lab head. If that does not settle it, a neutral senior person from outside the lab reads the evidence of who did what and makes a recommendation. Raise it early. Authorship disputes at submission are harder to fix than at the outline stage.

## Ideas and confidential material

Credit ideas that came from others in talks, papers and conversations. Do not use ideas, data or text from a manuscript or grant you are reviewing for anything but the review, and do not share it. Cite your sources properly, including preprints and datasets.
