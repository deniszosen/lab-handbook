# Research practice

## Plan before you start

For any experiment that will take more than a few days or use scarce material, write a short plan first: the question, the design, the controls, how many samples or animals and why that number, and how you will analyse the result. Discuss it with someone who is not doing the experiment.

Randomise and blind wherever the design allows it. When it does not, say so in the plan and say what you did instead. For confirmatory studies, write down the hypothesis and the analysis before you see the data, and write down which result would make you continue, change course or stop. Consider preregistration or a registered report when the study is large or the result will matter to decisions outside the lab.

## Notebooks

Every experiment goes in a notebook, electronic or paper, on the day you do it. Write enough that someone who was not there could repeat it: what you did, what you changed from the protocol, what went wrong, what you saw. Record the lot numbers of reagents. The lab head can read any notebook in the lab. Notebooks belong to the lab and stay when you go.

## Reagents and models

Validate antibodies for the application you use them for. Authenticate cell lines on arrival and at set intervals, and test cultures for mycoplasma on a schedule set by the person responsible for cell culture. Keep a register of strains, lines and key reagents, and use research resource identifiers (RRIDs) in papers where they exist.

We use human cells and tissue where they can answer the question, and animal or embryo models where they cannot. When animals are used, the work has approval before it starts. Check with your institution's animal welfare body whether the embryo stage you plan to use needs approval. Plan it with the PREPARE checklist, report it following ARRIVE 2.0, and apply the 3Rs: replace, reduce, refine.

## Human cell lines, stem cells and organoids

Know where every human line came from. For each one, record the donor source, the consent and approval that cover its use, the date it arrived, and any agreement that came with it. If you cannot show what a line may be used for, do not use it.

Authenticate lines by genotype, for example by STR profiling, on arrival and at set intervals. For stem cell lines, also check the karyotype and pluripotency at agreed passage numbers, and write the passage number in your notebook.

Finding the edit does not finish a genome-edited line. Confirm the edit by sequencing, check the karyotype, and use at least two independent clones, or say why you could not. Run the unedited parent line in the same experiment as the control.

Organoids vary between batches, between lines and from one organoid to the next. Before you start, decide what counts as one replicate: an independent differentiation, a line or a donor. Organoids from the same batch are not independent replicates, and the manuscript says what n refers to. For every batch, record the protocol version, the day of culture and a quality check, and plan for the batches that fail it.

Look at more than one independent readout before you call something a finding, and say so when you have only one. When an experiment applies a drug or chemical, record the concentration, the vehicle and the exposure time. Measure the concentration in the medium or the tissue when you can, and say how it relates to exposure in people.

## Analysis and statistics

Decide your exclusion rules before looking at outcomes. Do not remove an outlier because it is inconvenient. If you exclude data, say what you excluded and why. Report every analysis you ran that bears on a claim, including the ones that did not work. Show effect sizes and uncertainty alongside p-values. Ask a statistician when you are choosing a design, not after the data are in.

A second person reads analysis code that feeds a figure in a paper before submission. Code is covered in [chapter 8](08-data-code-and-records.md).

## Replication and checking

Before a key finding goes into a manuscript, a second person in the lab repeats it independently when that is feasible. When it is not feasible, the manuscript says so. Someone who did not work on a manuscript reads it before it leaves the lab.

## Images and figures

Keep the raw image files. Adjust brightness and contrast uniformly across the whole image and say so in the legend. Do not splice, clone, retouch or selectively sharpen. Mark any joining of separate images.

## When something turns out to be wrong

If you find an error in your own work or a colleague's, tell the person and the lab head. If it affects a published result, the lab corrects the record promptly, with a correction or a retraction. Finding your own error and reporting it is what a careful scientist does.

## AI tools

You may use AI tools for writing help, coding help and literature searching. You are responsible for everything you submit with your name on it. Check every citation and every line of generated code. Do not use AI to produce data, images of experimental results, or citations you have not verified. Do not paste unpublished results, partner data, personal data or confidential documents into external tools unless the lab head has approved that tool for that use. Follow the target journal's policy on disclosure and state in the methods or acknowledgements how a tool was used when the policy asks for it. AI tools are not authors.
