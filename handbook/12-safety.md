# Safety

Everyone is responsible for safety. The lab head is accountable for safe practice in the lab's own work. The host institution sets the safety rules and keeps the records, so use its training, its safety representative and its incident reporting.

## Stopping work

Anyone can stop any procedure they think is unsafe, including a visitor, a student and the lab head's own work. Nobody is penalised for stopping work in good faith. Safety concerns are reported on the day, and near misses are discussed in lab meetings without blame.

## Before you start

You do not work independently with a hazard until you have completed the required training and your supervisor has signed you off. Read the safety data sheet before using a chemical for the first time. A new procedure or a change to an old one gets a short risk assessment, written down and read by the lab head or the safety coordinator.

## Working alone and out of hours

Do not do hazardous work alone. That includes liquid nitrogen and work with viral vectors. If you must work outside normal hours, tell someone, agree a check-in time, and do only low-risk work. If you cannot agree a check-in, wait until the next working day.

## In the lab

Wear the protective equipment the risk assessment calls for. Tie back long hair and wear closed shoes. Do not eat, drink, store food or apply cosmetics in the lab. Label every container. Store chemicals according to their compatibility and put waste in the right stream. Report spills, injuries, exposures and equipment faults the same day.

Work with biological agents follows the approved containment level. Know the spill procedure before you start. Report any exposure immediately.

If you are pregnant, planning a pregnancy, immunocompromised or have another reason to change what you work with, you can talk to the lab head and to occupational health. The lab will adjust the risk assessment. You do not have to disclose more than you want to, and the conversation stays private.

## Hazards in our own work

Liquid nitrogen burns skin and can displace the air in a small room. Fill and open cryostorage with the gloves, face shield and oxygen monitoring that the risk assessment requires, and never alone. Use vials rated for the storage phase you choose.

Human cells and tissue are handled as potentially infectious, in a biosafety cabinet, at the containment level the risk assessment sets. Viral vectors and genome editing reagents need approved containment, decontamination with the right disinfectant, and a record of what was made. Check gas cylinders, CO2 incubators and centrifuges before use, and report faults the same day.

Some of the drugs and chemicals we study can harm development. Read the safety data sheet for reproductive toxicity before you handle any of them, and use the controls the risk assessment names. Anyone who is pregnant or planning a pregnancy can ask for that risk assessment at any time, as described above.

## Emergencies

In Norway, the emergency numbers are 110 for fire, 112 for police and 113 for an ambulance. Check that the building and your institution use the same ones.

Learn where the nearest exits, fire extinguisher, eyewash, safety shower and first aid kit are on your first day. Follow your institution's emergency procedures. When they differ from this handbook, they win.

## Visits to other sites

When you visit a hospital, a partner's lab or any other site, you follow its rules as well as ours. Before the first visit, the lab head briefs you on the site's safety rules, its confidentiality rules, and how to behave towards patients, staff and participants. Do not take photographs unless you have been told you may. Respect the privacy of anyone you meet there. Tell the lab head if you feel unsafe or are asked to do something that does not feel right, and leave if you need to.
